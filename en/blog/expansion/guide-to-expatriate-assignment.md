## The Key to a Successful Overseas Assignment — In 60 Seconds

What does it really take to make an overseas assignment succeed? In a word: thorough preparation, ongoing support, and deep cross-cultural understanding. This article breaks that success formula down into concrete, actionable steps.

We start with expatriate selection — explaining why soft skills like cross-cultural adaptability and communication matter just as much as technical expertise, and why family adaptability (often overlooked) is equally critical. We then examine the pre-departure and on-assignment support structures that help expatriates and their families settle smoothly, with a particular focus on mental health care and spousal career support.

From there, we cover the practical details of relocating — visas, housing, healthcare — plus strategies for recruiting and developing local staff, and the fundamentals of cross-cultural management. Real-world success stories are woven throughout. Read on to make your overseas expansion stick.

---

## Selecting the Right Expatriate: The Deciding Factor

The success or failure of an overseas assignment is largely determined by who you send. An employee who excels in Japan will not automatically excel abroad. Here's what to look for.

### Technical Skills Are Necessary, But Not Sufficient

The technical knowledge and hands-on experience to do the job are of course essential. Overseas postings rarely have the same dense training programs and detailed manuals as the Japan head office — expatriates must exercise independent judgment and manage their own work autonomously.

But these are merely necessary conditions, not sufficient ones. It's well documented that assignment failures are rarely caused by skill deficiencies alone. The real differentiators are soft skills.

### Soft Skills: The True Key to Success

- **Communication:** The ability to convey ideas clearly to local staff and customers, build consensus, and bring people along — this "mobilization" ability is indispensable.
- **Cross-cultural adaptability and flexibility:** The ability to openly accept a new environment and diverse value systems and adjust accordingly is arguably the single most important factor in overseas success.
- **Stress resilience:** The mental stability to face an unfamiliar environment, remain calm in the face of unexpected difficulty, and solve problems constructively.

These soft skills are repeatedly highlighted in research as critical predictors of expatriate success.

### A Holistic Selection Process — Including Family Adaptability

How do you find the right person? Look beyond technical capability alone and evaluate soft skills, mindset, and personal characteristics through a multidimensional process. Psychological assessments and assessment centers can help.

One factor that is frequently overlooked — yet critically important — is the adaptability of the family accompanying the expatriate. Research consistently identifies family maladaptation as one of the top causes of assignment failure. Build a process to evaluate the family's attitude toward overseas life and their capacity to adapt — for example, through family interviews. Given that a failed assignment can cost tens of millions of yen or more, investing in rigorous upfront selection pays significant dividends.

Notably, one study found that employees selected for their adventurous spirit — even those who hadn't originally sought an overseas posting — overwhelmingly reported feeling positive about the experience after arriving, citing personal growth. This suggests that individual adaptive qualities matter more than initial enthusiasm.

## Protecting the Wellbeing of Expatriates and Their Families

An overseas assignment's success doesn't rest solely on the expatriate. When family members are accompanying, whether the family adapts smoothly and maintains physical and mental stability has an enormous impact on the expatriate's performance and on the mission as a whole.

### Family Adaptation Is the Lifeline

As much research confirms, the adaptation of family — especially spouses and children — is a decisive factor in assignment success. When family members feel isolated or struggle to adapt locally, it creates serious psychological burdens for the expatriate, disrupts focus, and in the worst cases leads to early repatriation — a failure.

Common challenges include: spousal frustration over interrupted careers; children's education issues and difficulty adjusting to local schools; social isolation in a new country; and the mental health problems these can trigger. Companies need to recognize that their responsibility extends to meeting the needs of the entire family — not just the employee.

### Pre-Departure Support: Information and Wellbeing

Thorough preparation before departure is the foundation for a smooth transition for everyone.

- **Pre-departure counseling and information:** Provide accurate information on the local living environment, culture, healthcare system, and safety. Create space to share expectations and anxieties.
- **Children's education preparation:** Share information about local schools (Japanese schools, international schools) and support the selection process. Online tutoring services for overseas students can also help.
- **Spousal career support:** Consider career counseling for spouses, information about local work opportunities, and upskilling support. Studies suggest that approximately 70% of Japanese companies have no concrete program for this — a significant gap.

### On-Assignment Support: Ongoing Wellbeing

Company support must continue throughout the posting.

- **Housing and settling in:** Help secure a safe and comfortable home. Provide practical guidance on local transportation, daily services, etc.
- **Mental health care and stress management:** Set up a consultation channel and provide access to professional counseling. Mental stability for the entire family is essential to sustaining the expatriate's performance.
- **Building local community connections:** Facilitate interaction with other expatriate families and community activities to help build social connections.

A cautionary example: at one electronics manufacturer, the absence of family support had severe consequences. The expatriate was overwhelmed with work; his wife, having given up her career, struggled with isolation and the pressures of raising children abroad, and developed depression. The family repatriated mid-assignment, and the expatriate himself developed an adjustment disorder. Comprehensive, ongoing support structures are essential to avoid such outcomes.

## Practical Preparations for Life Abroad

Getting the day-to-day preparations right makes an enormous difference in settling in and quality of life. Here are the most commonly overlooked items to address before departure.

### Visas, Official Documentation, and Paperwork

- **Visas and passports:** Verify the validity of every family member's passport. Secure all necessary visas. Many supporting documents — family registers, certificates — must be arranged by the individual.
- **Overseas deregistration and resident registration:** For stays of one year or longer, file an overseas deregistration notice. This removes obligations for resident tax and national health insurance, but also limits access to some Japanese government services.
- **Driver's license:** Obtain an international driving permit. Check validity and renew the Japanese license if needed.

### Health First: Medical Preparation

- **Health and dental check-ups:** In addition to company-mandated pre-departure health checks, arrange comprehensive personal check-ups. Dental work in particular is often expensive overseas — handle it in Japan before you go.
- **Vaccinations:** Check the health and disease situation at your destination and plan vaccinations accordingly.
- **International health insurance:** Enroll in coverage with adequate benefit levels. Japanese national health insurance generally does not apply overseas.
- **Prescription medications:** Bring an adequate supply. Prepare English-language prescriptions and medical documentation as well.

### Banking and Communications

- **Bank accounts:** Confirm any service changes to your Japan account. Opening a local bank account for payroll and payments is standard.
- **Credit cards:** Prepare multiple internationally recognized cards, ideally with overseas travel insurance attached.
- **Mobile phone and internet:** Review your Japan plan; secure local communications (SIM card or new contract). Arrange home internet — note that in some countries like Germany, line installation takes significant time.

## Recruiting and Developing Local Staff

The success of an overseas base depends heavily on recruiting and developing strong local talent. Here's how to approach it.

### Define Who You Need and When

Don't recruit reactively — take a strategic approach. Clearly articulate what skills, experience, and qualities you need, how many people you need, and by when, based on the local base's mission and business targets.

Whether you're filling an immediate vacancy or making a strategic hire for medium-term growth determines your target profile and approach. Over-reliance on expatriates impedes true localization and inflates costs; plan a path toward local-led management.

### Write Job Postings That Attract the Right People

- **Clear, compelling job descriptions:** Don't just list tasks. Explain the role's importance in the organization, what outcomes are expected, and how the position contributes to company growth.
- **Communicate your culture and benefits:** Clearly convey your company's vision, values, workplace culture, growth opportunities, salary range, and benefits. Speak to candidates' aspirations.
- **Diversity and inclusion:** Signal that your organization respects diverse backgrounds and is committed to an inclusive workplace.

### Fair and Culturally Aware Selection Processes

- **Application review:** Resume formats vary by culture (e.g., whether photos are included). Evaluate candidates' actual skills and experience fairly, with cultural awareness.
- **Interviews:** Questions must comply with local labor laws and cultural norms. In many countries, questions about race, religion, or marital status are considered discriminatory and are legally prohibited. Use structured interviews (standardized questions and evaluation criteria) to ensure fairness.
- **Work eligibility check:** At the offer stage or an appropriate later point, confirm the candidate's right to work legally in the country.

**A real-world turnaround:** One Japanese manufacturer's Chinese subsidiary had struggled with poor sales for years. The company appointed a Chinese general manager who could internalize the Japan head office's philosophy and gave that person broad authority. A locally-led management team then developed new products tailored to Chinese market needs, achieving a dramatic V-shaped recovery — aided by a compensation system that reflected actual local performance.

## Cross-Cultural Management: Fundamentals for Global Leaders

In a globalized business environment, the ability to lead teams of people with diverse cultural backgrounds is no longer a "nice-to-have" — it's essential. Here are the core principles for turning cultural diversity into a strength.

### Self-Awareness and the Foundation of Cross-Cultural Understanding

Cross-cultural competence is now a mandatory capability. Key principles:

- **Self-awareness:** Deeply recognize that you yourself have been shaped by a specific culture and carry unconscious biases.
- **Respect for other cultures:** Accept and genuinely try to understand the other party's culture and values.
- **Flexibility and adaptability:** Adjust your own behavior and thinking to the situation, rather than clinging to your own cultural assumptions.
- **Empathy:** Actively try to understand the other party's perspective and cultural context.

### Communication Strategies for Diverse Teams

Many cross-cultural misunderstandings arise from communication breakdowns.

- **Language and non-verbal communication:** Even in a shared language (e.g., English), the nuances, cultural contexts, and non-verbal signals (gestures, eye contact) behind words are interpreted very differently across cultures.
- **Communication styles:** Some cultures communicate messages directly (low-context: e.g., the US, Germany); others rely heavily on reading between the lines and non-verbal cues (high-context: e.g., Japan, France). Being aware of these differences and adjusting your style is key.
- **Set clear communication rules:** Establish team norms for how to communicate, run meetings, and share information — this reduces misunderstanding and improves efficiency.

### Adapt Your Leadership Style — Locally

A leadership style that works well in your home culture may not transfer:

- **Attitudes toward authority:** How people show respect to leaders and what behaviors they expect differ by culture.
- **Decision-making styles:** Some cultures favor fast, top-down decisions; others prioritize building consensus among stakeholders.
- **Cultural agility:** Effective global leaders exercise "cultural agility" — reading team members' cultural backgrounds and situations holistically, then adapting their leadership style accordingly.

### Constructive Conflict Resolution in Diverse Teams

Conflict is inevitable in culturally diverse teams. What matters is how you frame it and handle it.

- **Attitudes toward conflict:** Some cultures see open conflict as healthy debate; others see it as something to avoid in the interest of harmony.
- **Resolution approach:** Practice calm dialogue and active listening. Seek to understand the other party's perspective and cultural context. When needed, a cultural mediator (go-between) can help.

McDonald's is well known for developing menus that respect local food cultures and religious contexts (e.g., the "Maharaja Mac" in India). Coca-Cola, finding that its original brand name sounded inappropriate in Chinese, changed to "可口可乐" (Kě Kǒu Kě Lè — "tasty and enjoyable") — an auspicious-sounding phonetic rendering — with great success. These cases illustrate the power of deeply understanding and adapting to local culture.
