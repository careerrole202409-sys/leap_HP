For business leaders based in Singapore or elsewhere in Southeast Asia, breaking into the Japanese market demands more than a solid product and a clear strategy. It demands communication — and specifically, Japanese communication that reads as genuine, not machine-translated. Whether the goal is hiring Japanese talent, building client relationships, or establishing credibility as a brand, the ability to publish in Japanese that sounds like *you* has become a real competitive differentiator.

Hiromi Ishide is building a product to solve exactly that problem. She founded a social media consultancy in Japan at the height of the pandemic, spent years helping executives find their voice on LinkedIn, and eventually relocated to Singapore — where she realized that the gap in Japanese-language communication tools was even wider than she'd thought.

We spoke with Ishide about her entrepreneurial path, the AI platform she's developing, and what she believes global business leaders consistently miss when they try to communicate with Japan.

## "Start With What You Can Do" — The Origin of Her Entrepreneurial Journey

**— How did you first get into entrepreneurship?**

I launched CyberReach Inc. in 2020, right as COVID was disrupting everything. I had skills in web marketing, so I started there — not from some sweeping vision, but from a very practical place: what can I actually do right now?

From that starting point, I spent years working with founders and executives on their social media presence and LinkedIn strategy. What kept striking me was this: I'd sit across from accomplished, experienced leaders — typically men in their 40s and 50s — who had real expertise and genuine stories to tell, and yet they were stuck. "I don't know what to write." "I can't keep it up." "I can't find my own voice." People with enormous professional value, blocked at the point of expression.

My approach was never about chasing follower counts or engineering viral moments. I always worked backwards from the goal: Are you trying to strengthen your hiring pipeline? Build inroads with new clients? Once that was clear, everything else followed. And seeing clients come back and say "this actually worked" was what kept me going.

## Hitting the Ceiling of Human-Only Support — and Turning Toward AI

**— What drove you toward building an AI product?**

One-on-one consulting and content management doesn't scale. There's a hard ceiling on how many people I can genuinely support at one time. That constraint was always there.

And then I started noticing something while scrolling through LinkedIn: by some estimates, more than half of the long-form English posts on the platform are now AI-generated. You can sense it — the volume has clearly surged, and so has a certain flatness in how things read.

That's the core problem: AI makes it easy to produce a lot of content, but so much of it sounds generic. Different people, same voice. The actual thinking and perspective of the person behind the post gets lost.

I think we're heading toward a clear split in how AI gets used for content: either "fast, high-volume, and forgettable" — or "AI-assisted and distinctly yours, where your actual vision and values come through." The second approach is where the real value lies. And I couldn't find a product that delivered it. So I decided to build one.

## The Service: Japanese-Language Post Generation

**— What exactly are you building?**

An AI platform for overseas business leaders and executives who want to establish a presence in the Japanese market through LinkedIn — specifically, by publishing Japanese-language content that actually resonates with a Japanese audience.

The flow is straightforward. After signing up, users complete a short onboarding where they define their communication goals: what they're trying to achieve by putting themselves out there publicly. From there, the app opens with a simple question: "What's something that happened recently?" You might enter something like, "I had a conversation with a client yesterday and came away thinking differently about something." The AI then generates three to five follow-up questions — "What specifically surprised you?" "What did you take away from that exchange?" — and your answers drive the final output: three Japanese post drafts to choose from.

The design is built to draw out what you genuinely think and feel, then shape that into language that lands with a Japanese audience. This is fundamentally different from copying text into ChatGPT and asking for a Japanese translation. We're not starting from text — we're starting from the person. The whole process is articulation support, not translation. The Japanese post that comes out should read like *you* wrote it.

The methodology comes from two years of my own experimentation in my consulting work — iterating on questioning frameworks and prompting structures until I understood what actually unlocks someone's authentic voice. This platform is that process, systematized.

## Why "Communicable Japanese" Is the Deciding Factor for Japan Market Entry

**— Why does the quality of Japanese communication matter so much?**

Whether you're hiring or selling into Japan, the final decision comes down to language. A post can be accurate and well-structured, but if it reads as flat or generic — the same as anything else on the feed — it doesn't move anyone.

This is especially true for overseas companies trying to reach Japanese audiences. It's not about grammatically correct Japanese. It's about Japanese that conveys genuine intent. Take a company based in Singapore that's trying to recruit Japanese talent: the difference between sending recruiter DMs and having the company's founder publish consistent Japanese-language posts about their management philosophy, company values, and day-to-day thinking — that difference is enormous. The DMs get ignored. The posts build trust over months, and trust changes application rates.

The same dynamic plays out in sales. When a Japanese buyer reads something and senses that the perspective behind it is real — that it wasn't generated in bulk — it influences the final call in ways that are hard to quantify but very real.

## "Leaving Something Behind" Over Scaling Up

**— What's your longer-term vision?**

My core motivation is preserving language that deserves to survive.

Japanese is more than a communication medium — it carries a culture. If the AI tools that exist continue to treat it as just another output language, the number of people who make the effort to communicate in Japanese will keep declining. The language risks being hollowed out over time. That's a long-horizon risk, but one I think is worth working against now.

So if there are leaders anywhere in the world who genuinely want to communicate in Japanese — who have something real to say and want to say it in a form that truly reaches people — I want to build the infrastructure for that. Getting the right tool to the right people matters more to me than raw scale.

We're planning to launch on July 5, 2026, beginning with an invite-only group. We'll iterate based on that feedback before opening more broadly.

## What Leaders Outside Japan Consistently Miss About Japanese Communication

**— What advice would you give to an overseas business leader trying to build a presence in Japan?**

From what I've seen, the biggest mistake is treating Japan communication as a translation problem. Leaders have compelling content in English, they convert it to Japanese, and wonder why it doesn't land. Japan audiences read differently — credibility signals are different. A polished generic post doesn't build the same trust it might in other markets.

What actually works is consistent, personal communication over time. Not a single post, not a campaign — a sustained cadence of genuine perspective shared in language that sounds like a real person wrote it. For hiring, a Japanese candidate who has followed your founder's posts for six months is in a completely different headspace than someone who received a cold DM. For sales, a Japanese buyer who has been tracking your thinking already feels like they know you before the first meeting.

LinkedIn makes this possible without requiring fluency. The tools to do it well are improving. The real barrier isn't language ability — it's clarity of message. Knowing what you want to say, and who you're saying it to. Once that's clear, the rest is solvable.
