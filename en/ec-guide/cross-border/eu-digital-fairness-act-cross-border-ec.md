## Quick Overview: What Is the EU Digital Fairness Act Proposal?

The EU Digital Fairness Act (DFA) is a proposed EU regulation that aims to give consumers the same level of protection online as offline. It would ban dark patterns (interface designs that trick or pressure consumers into buying or signing up) and make subscriptions easier to cancel, among other things. The European Commission published the proposal on November 11, 2026.

- **Where it stands**: The Commission has tabled the proposal. It now goes to the European Parliament and the Council, and its content may change
- **Does it affect sellers outside the EU?**: Yes. Traders who target consumers in the EU are covered even if they are established outside the EU. A companion proposal would also require non-EU traders to appoint a legal representative in the EU
- **Key measures**: A ban on fake "only 1 left" claims, an obligation to include unavoidable fees and taxes in the first price shown, active consent before a free trial turns into a paid plan, an online cancellation button, and clear labeling of influencer ads
- **When it applies**: The proposal says it would apply 12 months after entry into force. There is no date yet for adoption

This article explains what cross-border ecommerce sellers need to know, based on the Commission's announcement and the text of the proposals. Information is current as of November 27, 2026.

## The Digital Fairness Act Proposal at a Glance

On November 11, 2026, the European Commission presented three proposals together as a "Consumer Package." The Commission describes it as the most significant upgrade of EU consumer protection legislation in over 20 years.

| Proposal | What it does |
|---|---|
| Digital Fairness Act (DFA) | Replaces the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (UCPD) with a regulation that applies directly in every Member State, and adds new rules on dark patterns, pricing, subscriptions, and more |
| Simplification Directive | Amends the Consumer Rights Directive to simplify pre-contractual information for repeat in-app purchases and similar cases |
| Revision of the Consumer Protection Cooperation (CPC) Regulation | Lets the Commission directly investigate and fine large-scale breaches, and requires non-EU traders to appoint a legal representative |

Because the UCPD is a directive, each Member State has transposed it into its own national law. The DFA is a regulation, so once adopted, the same rules would apply directly across all Member States. The Commission says this will save businesses the cost of navigating different national rules.

## When Will the Digital Fairness Act Apply?

No application date has been set yet. Under the proposal, the regulation would enter into force on the twentieth day after publication in the Official Journal of the EU and apply 12 months later. The CPC revision also proposes to apply 12 months after entry into force.

1. November 11, 2026: The Commission publishes the proposal
2. The European Parliament and the Council debate and amend it
3. Once adopted, it is published in the Official Journal and enters into force on the twentieth day
4. It applies 12 months after entry into force (as currently proposed)

Since the content and timeline may change during negotiations, treat it for now as a clear signal of direction rather than a final set of obligations.

## Does the Digital Fairness Act Apply to Sellers Outside the EU?

Yes. If you sell to consumers in the EU from Japan or anywhere else, the Digital Fairness Act is relevant to you.

### Non-EU traders are in scope

The proposal's explanatory memorandum notes that the current UCPD applies to any trader targeting consumers in the EU, including traders established outside the EU, and that the DFA reproduces the UCPD's scope. If you sell to EU consumers through your own online store, it is safest to assume you are covered.

### Appointing a legal representative in the EU

The CPC revision would require traders that have no establishment in the EU but target consumers in one or more Member States to designate, in writing, a legal representative in one of those Member States. The representative's name, postal address, email address, and phone number must be publicly available on the tools you use to reach consumers, such as your website. The proposal gives a subsidiary or parent company established in the EU as one example of a suitable representative. The article on legal representatives contains no exemption for small businesses.

### Fines for breaches

Fines depend on who enforces the rules.

| Enforcer | Scope | Maximum fine |
|---|---|---|
| European Commission (direct enforcement) | Large-scale breaches affecting consumers in at least two-thirds of Member States, representing at least two-thirds of the EU population. SMEs are excluded | Up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover |
| National authorities (in CPC coordinated actions) | Breaches spanning several Member States | Member States must set the maximum at no less than 4% of annual turnover in the Member States concerned |

As an illustrative calculation: if a company with annual EU sales of JPY 100 million were targeted by a CPC coordinated action for a breach spanning several Member States, the maximum fine would have to be set at JPY 4 million or more (assuming those sales are in the Member States concerned). SMEs are outside the Commission's direct enforcement, but they remain subject to national enforcement.

## What the Digital Fairness Act Bans and Requires

For online stores, the main areas are dark patterns, pricing, subscriptions, influencer marketing, and email consent.

### The dark pattern blacklist (10 practices)

An annex to the proposal lists ten dark patterns that would be considered unfair in all circumstances.

| Banned design | Ecommerce example |
|---|---|
| False claims about availability or popularity, or hiding relevant qualifications | Untrue "only 1 left" or "5 others are looking at this" |
| Persistent availability or popularity messages throughout checkout to create scarcity | "Almost gone" displayed on every step from cart to payment |
| Default options the consumer has to reject | Pre-ticked paid add-ons, products added to the cart automatically |
| Presenting the option to decline as the wrong choice | An unsubscribe button that says "No thanks, I don't like good deals" |
| Creating the false impression there is no other option | Making it look as if only the highlighted plan can be chosen |
| Repeatedly asking for a choice that has already been made | A sign-up prompt that keeps reappearing after being declined |
| Misleading or ambiguous wording, including double negatives | Options like "Don't opt out of not receiving offers" |
| Showing price, contract duration, or auto-renewal less prominently | Auto-renewal terms in tiny print |
| Notifications that falsely suggest someone contacted you or action is needed | A fake "You have a new message from the store" |
| Making refusing, withdrawing, or changing a choice disproportionately hard | One-click sign-up, but cancellation buried several pages deep |

The Commission would be able to add to or modify this list in light of market developments and enforcement experience.

### Pricing: show unavoidable costs in the first price

The Digital Fairness Act would require traders to include all foreseeable fees, taxes, and charges that the consumer will unavoidably incur in the price first shown. This effectively bans drip pricing, where extra fees only appear at checkout.

According to the proposal's recitals, the ban applies where a fee cannot be avoided and its amount is known in advance, such as a fixed delivery fee. It does not apply to charges that depend on details only set later in the ordering process, such as delivery costs that vary by address, order total, or delivery method. Because shipping often varies by destination country in cross-border ecommerce, it is worth sorting out which of your charges count as "known in advance."

Misleading price comparisons, such as using a reference price to make a discount look bigger than it really is, would also be covered.

### Subscriptions: free trials, auto-renewals, and a cancellation button

Sellers offering subscriptions or recurring orders would face new obligations:

- **Free trials**: Remind consumers before the trial ends of the end date, the price after the trial, and how to cancel. The consumer is not bound by a paid contract unless they expressly agree
- **Auto-renewal (contracts of six months or longer)**: Send a reminder between one month and 14 days before renewal with the renewal date, price, and cancellation terms
- **Auto-renewal (contracts shorter than six months)**: Send a reminder at least one week before the renewal that brings the total duration to six months or is the tenth renewal, whichever comes first
- **Cancellation button**: If contracts can be concluded online, provide a prominent cancellation function labeled "terminate the contract here" or similar
- **Access to a human**: In online customer service after a contract is concluded, offer communication with a human being on request when other means are not effective

### Influencer marketing and social media

When using influencer marketing, each commercial post must clearly state that it is an "advertisement" or a "commercial collaboration." Brands that hire influencers must also ensure, by contract or other means, that the influencers comply.

### Personalised ads and prices

Today, the bans on profiling-based ads aimed at minors and ads based on sensitive data such as health apply only to online platforms. The Digital Fairness Act would extend them to all websites and apps. Presenting higher personalised prices to minors or vulnerable consumers would also be banned.

### Consent for email marketing

Direct marketing by email or SMS generally requires the consumer's prior consent. As an exception, you may email customers about your own similar products using contact details obtained during a sale, provided they can object free of charge and easily both when the details are collected and in every message.

## What to Review on Your Online Store Now

The Digital Fairness Act is still a proposal, but many of the blacklisted practices could already raise issues under current EU consumer law. Based on the text of the proposals, we have put together a checklist for cross-border stores selling to the EU.

| Where to check | What to review | Related provision |
|---|---|---|
| Product pages | Do stock and "people viewing" counters match real data? | Dark pattern blacklist |
| Product listings and pages | Are fixed fees and flat-rate shipping included in the first price shown? | Drip pricing ban |
| Cart and checkout | Are any paid add-ons pre-ticked? | Dark pattern blacklist |
| Newsletter sign-up and unsubscribe | Do you have records of consent? Does the unsubscribe button shame the user? | Email consent, dark pattern blacklist |
| Subscription sign-up and account page | Is there a cancellation button? Can you send pre-renewal reminders? | Cancellation function, renewal reminders |
| Social media and influencer campaigns | Do your contracts require an "advertisement" label on sponsored posts? | Influencer marketing |
| Company and legal information pages | Is there a place to list your legal representative's details? | CPC revision |

If you email overseas customers, keep a record of where and with what consent each contact was collected. ## FAQ

**Q. What is the Digital Fairness Act (DFA)?**

A. The Digital Fairness Act is a proposed EU regulation, published by the European Commission on November 11, 2026, that bans dark patterns and makes subscriptions easier to cancel, among other measures. It would replace the current Unfair Commercial Practices Directive with a regulation and add rules for problems specific to the online world.

**Q. When will the Digital Fairness Act's dark pattern rules apply?**

A. No date has been set yet. The proposal says it would apply 12 months after entry into force, but negotiations in the European Parliament and the Council have only just begun, so the timing and content may change.

**Q. Does the Digital Fairness Act apply to companies outside the EU?**

A. Yes, if they sell to consumers in the EU. The explanatory memorandum says traders established outside the EU are covered when they target EU consumers, and the CPC revision would also require them to appoint a legal representative in the EU.

**Q. How much are the fines under the Digital Fairness Act?**

A. For large-scale breaches enforced directly by the Commission, fines could reach 6% of worldwide annual turnover. SMEs are excluded from direct Commission enforcement, but in national enforcement through CPC coordinated actions, Member States must set maximum fines at no less than 4% of annual turnover in the Member States concerned.

## Summary and Key Takeaway

The Digital Fairness Act proposal would ban, across the whole EU, interface designs that shoppers often run into online, such as fake "only 1 left" claims, pre-ticked add-ons, and hard-to-find cancellation flows. Non-EU traders are in scope and would likely need to appoint a legal representative in the EU.

The bigger picture is that for cross-border ecommerce into the EU, regulation now covers not only what you sell and how you ship it, but also how your store's interface is designed. Adoption is still some way off, but stock messages, price displays, and cancellation flows are often copied straight from a store built for the home market. If you sell to the EU, keep the proposal's blacklist at hand and review your store's design before you expand further. That is the surest way to avoid scrambling later.
