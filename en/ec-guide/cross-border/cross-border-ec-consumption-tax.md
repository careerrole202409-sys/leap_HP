## Quick Overview: Selling Overseas Qualifies for Japan's Export Tax Exemption

When a Japan-based business sells goods to consumers overseas through cross-border ecommerce, that sale qualifies for Japan's consumption tax export exemption, meaning no consumption tax applies to the sale itself. At the same time, the consumption tax paid on related purchases remains eligible for input tax credit, and any amount left over after that credit can be refunded. This guide walks through the scope of the export exemption, the documentation it requires, and how it interacts with the Invoice System, based on guidance published by Japan's National Tax Agency (NTA). Because actual tax treatment varies by business, this is written on the assumption that specific decisions go through a tax accountant.

## What the Export Exemption Covers

Per the NTA's Tax Answer "No. 6551: Exemption for Export Transactions," a taxable business is exempt from consumption tax on transactions including:

- Transfers or leases of assets as exports from Japan
- International communications, postal, and courier services between Japan and abroad
- Transfers or leases of intangible property rights (patents, copyrights, etc.) to non-residents
- Services provided to non-residents — except services a non-resident directly benefits from within Japan, such as domestic shipping/storage or food and lodging, which remain taxable

When a cross-border ecommerce business ships goods to an overseas consumer, that counts as a transfer of assets as an export, so it qualifies for the exemption. One note from the same page: starting October 1, 2026, sales, exchanges, leases, and brokerage of domestic real estate to non-residents are no longer export-exempt (contracts signed by March 31, 2026 are grandfathered under the old rules). That doesn't directly affect product sales, but it's a reminder that the scope of this exemption can shift with tax reform.

## How Input Tax Credit and Refunds Work

The export exemption only applies to the consumption tax on sales — it doesn't remove your right to claim input tax credit on your purchases. Per the same NTA page, consumption tax included in taxable purchases tied to your export transactions (inventory, packaging, advertising, and so on) remains eligible for input tax credit when you file. Since your sales-side tax is zero while your purchase-side tax is still creditable, any credit you can't use gets refunded.

That said, under the Qualified Invoice System (Japan's version of an invoice-based VAT system), claiming that input tax credit generally requires keeping qualified invoices issued by your suppliers.

## Documentation Required to Claim the Export Exemption

Per the same NTA page, the documentation required to claim the export exemption depends on the type of transaction, and all of it must be retained for seven years:

- Goods requiring customs export declaration: the customs-certified export permit (electronic records count)
- Postal exports valued over ¥200,000: the customs-certified export permit
- Postal exports valued at ¥200,000 or less (parcels, EMS): a receipt from Japan Post, plus copies of shipping documents listing the exporter, item names, quantities, values, the recipient, and the acceptance date
- Postal exports valued at ¥200,000 or less (ordinary mail): a Japan Post acceptance receipt listing item names, quantities, and values

Per the same page, value is generally assessed on an FOB basis, judged per package, with multiple packages to the same recipient added together.

## How the Invoice System Affects Cross-Border Sellers

The Invoice System itself doesn't determine whether you qualify for the export exemption, but it does affect how much input tax credit you can claim on your purchases. Per the NTA's April 2026 release, "On the Fiscal 2026 Tax Reform Related to the Invoice System," there's a transitional measure that lets businesses treat a set percentage of purchases from non-qualified-invoice issuers (including tax-exempt businesses) as creditable consumption tax. The fiscal 2026 reform extended this measure by two years and revised the applicable percentages. As of November 2026, the schedule is:

- October 1, 2026 - September 30, 2028: 70%
- October 1, 2028 - September 30, 2030: 50%
- October 1, 2030 - September 30, 2031: 30%
- From October 1, 2031: 0% (the transitional measure ends)

The same release also notes that if your total purchases from non-qualified-invoice issuers exceed ¥100 million in a given year or fiscal year (down from ¥1 billion before the reform), the portion above that threshold no longer qualifies for this transitional measure (effective for fiscal periods starting on or after October 1, 2026).

In practice, if your suppliers — manufacturers, wholesalers, logistics providers, and so on — are tax-exempt businesses, you may only be able to claim input tax credit within the limits of this transitional measure. Confirming whether your suppliers are registered as qualified invoice issuers is a practical step worth taking.

## FAQ

**Q. Does consumption tax apply to sales made to overseas consumers?**

A. Generally, no. When a Japan-based business exports goods to consumers overseas, that sale qualifies for the consumption tax export exemption.

**Q. Do I need to file anything special to claim the export exemption?**

A. No special application is required, but you do need to retain documentation proving the export — such as an export permit or a Japan Post receipt — for seven years. Without it, the exemption may not be recognized.

**Q. What happens to the consumption tax I paid on my purchases?**

A. Consumption tax included in taxable purchases tied to your export transactions remains eligible for input tax credit, and any amount you can't use gets refunded. Under the Invoice System, claiming that credit generally requires keeping qualified invoices.

**Q. What if my supplier is a tax-exempt business?**

A. Purchases from a tax-exempt business that can't issue qualified invoices generally aren't fully creditable. As of November 2026, a transitional measure lets you treat 70% of the purchase amount as creditable through September 2028, with that percentage declining in stages until the measure ends in October 2031.

**Q. Can I handle the refund filing myself?**

A. The basic mechanics are publicly documented, but documentation requirements and how they interact with the Invoice System can get business-specific. For an accurate filing, it's worth consulting a tax accountant.

## Summary and Key Takeaway

The core logic of consumption tax in cross-border ecommerce is straightforward once you see it: sales are exempt under the export exemption, while purchases remain eligible for credit and refund. Where it gets more involved is the documentation required to claim the exemption and the percentages and deadlines tied to the Invoice System's transitional measure — both of which can shift with tax reform, so it's worth checking the NTA's latest guidance before you file. Because accuracy matters so much in this area, treat this as a starting point and bring the specifics to a tax accountant.
